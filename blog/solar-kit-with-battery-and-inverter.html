Solar Panel Kit With Battery and Inverter: Will It Run Your Home?

By The Off-Grid Workshop

A kit's title tells you panel watts. Whether it runs your home depends on four other numbers. Here is how to check them against your own loads before you buy.

Quick Answer

Compare four figures on the listing with four figures from your power audit:

Kit figureCompare withSimple test
Panel wattsYour daily energy (Wh/day)Panel W × worst-month peak sun hours × 0.75 ≥ daily Wh
Battery energy (Wh)Energy used between sunset and sunrise, plus cloudy daysBattery Wh × usable fraction × 0.9 ≥ energy to cover
Inverter continuous and surge wattsLoads running together, and the biggest motor startContinuous ≥ running load + 25%; surge ≥ running load + largest start
Charge controller type and ampsArray watts and battery voltageMPPT amps ≥ panel W ÷ battery V

The 0.75 and 0.9 factors are planning allowances for losses, not equipment ratings; replace them with figures for your equipment if you have them.

1. Will the Panels Cover Your Daily Energy?

Daily energy delivered ≈ panel watts × peak sun hours × (1 − losses). Peak sun hours change with season and location; design for the lowest month the system must carry alone. Get monthly figures for your site from NREL's PVWatts.

All rows use a 25% combined loss allowance.

2. How Long Will the Battery Last?

Battery capacity is only comparable in watt-hours. Amp-hours need a voltage: 100Ah at 12.8V is 1,280Wh, but 100Ah at 51.2V is 5,120Wh, four times as much. If a listing gives only Ah, multiply by the battery's nominal voltage.

Not all of that energy reaches your appliances. Allow for the usable window the battery maker permits and for battery and inverter losses: with an 80% usable window and 90% delivery efficiency, a 5,120Wh battery delivers about 3,690Wh. Compare that with what you use overnight, and with how many cloudy days you want to ride through before running a generator or cutting loads.

Lithium iron phosphate (LiFePO4) batteries generally must not be charged below freezing unless they have built-in heating. Check the charging temperature range on the battery data sheet if the battery will be in an unheated space.

3. Can the Inverter Handle Your Loads?

  • Continuous rating: add the AC loads that can run at the same time, then add headroom; 25% is a common planning margin.
  • Surge rating and duration: fridges, freezers, pumps, compressors and power tools draw several times their running power for a moment when they start. The inverter must supply the running load plus the largest start, for as long as the start lasts. Check both the surge watts and the stated surge time.
  • Standby (no-load) power: an inverter left on all day uses energy even with nothing plugged in. 25W for 24 hours is 600Wh, which comes out of your daily energy budget.
  • Output voltage: confirm it matches your appliances (for example 120V in the US) and whether it can supply 240V loads if you have them.

4. Check the System Voltage and Currents

The same power at a lower voltage means more current, thicker cables and larger fuses. Approximate battery current drawn by an inverter at 90% efficiency:

Inverter output12.8V battery25.6V battery51.2V battery
1,000W87A43A22A
2,000W174A87A43A
3,000W260A130A65A

Compare these with the battery's maximum continuous discharge current (its BMS limit) and with the cable and fuse sizes in the inverter manual. Current rises further as the battery voltage falls toward its cut-off. As a rule of thumb, larger home systems usually move to 24V or 48V for this reason.

5. Is the Charge Controller Matched?

  • MPPT charge current ≈ panel watts ÷ battery voltage. 1,200W on a 51.2V battery is about 23A; on a 12.8V battery it is about 94A.
  • Maximum PV input voltage: panel voltage rises in cold weather. The cold-morning open-circuit voltage of each series string must stay below the controller's limit.
  • PWM controllers need panels whose operating voltage suits the battery and waste more energy; many budget kits use them.
  • All-in-one units combine inverter and MPPT controller. Check the PV input limits of the combined unit in the same way.

6. What Is Usually Not in the Box

  • Overcurrent protection (fuses or breakers) close to the battery, and PV and battery disconnects, as your design and local code require.
  • Cables long enough for your actual layout, sized for both current and voltage drop.
  • Mounting suited to your roof or ground and rated for local wind and snow.
  • Grounding and bonding materials, labels, and any permits or inspections your jurisdiction requires.
  • A battery monitor, if you want an accurate state of charge.

Worked Example

An illustrative household uses 3,293Wh per day (including 600Wh of inverter standby), has 2,294W of loads that can run together and a 3,000W startup from a power tool. It is considering a hypothetical kit with 1,200W of panels, a 51.2V 100Ah battery, a 3,000W inverter and a 60A MPPT controller.

  • Panels: in a 5.5 peak-sun-hour summer month, 1,200 × 5.5 × 0.75 = 4,950Wh, enough. In a 2.3-hour winter month, 1,200 × 2.3 × 0.75 = 2,070Wh, about 1,200Wh short every day. Winter needs a larger array, lower loads or a generator.
  • Battery: 5,120Wh × 0.8 × 0.9 ≈ 3,690Wh delivered, about 1.1 days of this load.
  • Inverter: 2,294W × 1.25 = 2,868W continuous, so 3,000W passes. The peak during a tool start is 2,294 + 3,000 = 5,294W: check the inverter's surge rating and duration, or avoid starting the tool while other large loads run.
  • Controller: 1,200W ÷ 51.2V ≈ 23A, well within 60A. The string voltage still needs the cold-weather check.

The kit suits this household from spring to autumn but not in winter, which is exactly the kind of result a listing's headline numbers hide.

Frequently Asked Questions

How big a solar kit do I need to run a house?

Work from your daily energy use and your worst-month sun. A household using 3,300Wh a day needs about 1,000W of panels in a 4.5-peak-sun-hour month but about 1,900W in a 2.3-hour month, with 25% losses and before any reserve. Many full-time homes use far more energy than this, so start with a power audit.

Is 100Ah enough for a solar kit?

It depends on the voltage. 100Ah at 12.8V is 1,280Wh, roughly 920Wh delivered to appliances after a typical usable window and losses. The same 100Ah at 51.2V is 5,120Wh. Always compare batteries in watt-hours.

Can a solar kit with an inverter run a refrigerator?

Usually, if the inverter's surge rating covers the compressor start on top of the other running loads, and the panels and battery cover the fridge's daily energy, which you can measure with a plug-in energy meter over 48 hours.

Should I choose a 12V, 24V or 48V kit?

Small systems for vans, RVs and sheds often use 12V. For inverters above about 1,000 to 2,000W, 24V or 48V keeps battery currents, cable sizes and fuse ratings manageable. Choose equipment that is all designed for the same system voltage.
