Why Getting the Size Right Matters

Undersizing your solar system means you run out of power by evening. Oversizing means you spent thousands of dollars on panels and batteries you’ll never use. The sweet spot is a system sized to meet your actual load with a reasonable safety margin. and that requires math, not guesswork.

The good news: the math is straightforward. You need four numbers: your daily energy consumption in watt-hours, your location’s peak sun hours, a panel array size, and a battery bank capacity. Use the solar sizing calculator, then follow the worked steps below.

Before You Begin

This guide assumes you’ve already completed a power audit and know your estimated daily watt-hour consumption. If you haven’t done that yet, start there. The number you get from your power audit is the foundation of everything in this guide.

Step 1: Establish Your Daily Energy Use

Start with the power audit: watts multiplied by hours of operation gives watt-hours (Wh). Add the daily energy for all your appliances, including standby consumption. A 100W appliance running for three hours uses 300Wh; this does not tell you its startup power.

This guide uses a hypothetical 1,500Wh/day load. The calculator starts with a 25% combined solar-to-load loss allowance. Replace it with a loss budget for your equipment. A 25% loss means dividing the load by 0.75, not multiplying by 1.25. Do not add the same inverter or battery losses a second time to this combined allowance.

Step 2: Find Your Design-Month Peak Sun Hours

Peak sun hours express daily solar energy as equivalent hours at 1,000W/m². They are not daylight hours. Use location-specific monthly data for the planned panel orientation, and design for the lowest-sun month when the system must operate.

The example uses 3 peak sun hours as an input assumption, not a regional guarantee. Monthly averages do not predict a particular cloudy spell. Seasonal loads, shading and backup charging also affect a workable design.

Step 3: Calculate the Solar Array

Array target (W) = Daily Wh ÷ [Peak sun hours × (1 − loss fraction)] × (1 + reserve fraction)

For this example: 1,500 ÷ (3 × 0.75) × 1.20 = 800W. Two 400W panels meet that arithmetic target. The 20% reserve is a planning choice, separate from the loss allowance.

Round up to whole panels, then check the controller’s input voltage, current and power limits. Meeting the daily energy target does not guarantee rapid recovery after several cloudy days. Additional array capacity, reduced loads or backup generation may be needed.

Step 4: Size the Battery Bank

Nominal bank capacity (Wh) = Daily Wh × Days without solar ÷ (Usable discharge fraction × Battery-to-load efficiency)

The battery calculation starts from appliance energy again, so it includes delivery losses from the battery to the loads. It does not use the combined solar loss allowance from the array calculation.

With two days of storage, 80% usable discharge and 90% delivery efficiency: 1,500 × 2 ÷ (0.80 × 0.90) = 4,167Wh nominal. Choose a bank with at least this rated energy, then allow for temperature, ageing and the manufacturer’s operating limits.

Bank Ah = Nominal Wh ÷ Nominal bank voltage. At 25.6V the example needs at least 163Ah. At 51.2V the same energy needs at least 82Ah. Use the battery data sheet’s nominal voltage rather than its charging voltage.

Series and Parallel Do Not Create Extra Energy

Three 12V, 100Ah batteries contain 3 × 12 × 100 = 3,600Wh nominal. In series they form a 36V, 100Ah bank; in parallel they form a 12V, 300Ah bank. Neither arrangement provides 6,000Wh. Connections must follow the battery manufacturer’s instructions and match the inverter.

Step 5: Check Continuous and Startup Power Separately

Daily Wh cannot determine inverter watts. Add the AC loads that can run simultaneously, and check the total load while a motor or compressor starts. The calculator uses 25% continuous headroom as a planning assumption.

A 1,000W simultaneous running load gives a 1,250W continuous target. If the total startup load is 2,500W, the inverter must also support that peak for the equipment’s required startup duration. Check watts, VA/power factor, ambient-temperature derating and battery/BMS current limits against the actual equipment specifications.
