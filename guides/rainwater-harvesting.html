Rainwater Harvesting 101: Collect, Store & Filter

Plan Around Supply, Demand and Intended Use

A roof can supply water for some household or garden uses, but rainfall, storage and permitted uses determine whether the system suits your property. Compare collection with demand through dry periods before choosing equipment. A large annual rainfall total does not guarantee water when you need it.

For drinking, cooking and other uses where water may be swallowed, collected rainwater needs a separate safety assessment. Clear-looking water can contain germs or chemicals. CDC recommends using public-system tap water or bottled water for drinking, cooking, brushing teeth, rinsing produce and watering edible plants where possible. See CDC rainwater guidance.

Step 1: Calculate Your Catchment Potential

Estimated collected gallons = horizontal catchment area (sq ft) × rainfall (inches) × 0.623 × collection efficiency

The factor 0.623 is an approximate US-gallon unit conversion, not an allowance for losses. Apply efficiency separately for water lost from collection. Use the horizontal area feeding the connected gutters, rather than the sloped roof surface. DOE uses this approach with a rounded 0.62 conversion in its system-sizing guidance.

Illustrative Calculation, Not a Site Survey

  • Connected roof footprint: 1,200 sq ft; rainfall: 1 inch.
  • Gross rainfall volume: 1,200 × 1 × 0.623 = 747.6 gallons.
  • Assumed collection efficiency: 0.80, selected for this example.
  • Estimated collection: 747.6 × 0.80 = 598.1 gallons, before any overflow from insufficient tank space.

With 30 inches of rain, the same assumptions give 22,428 gross gallons or about 17,942 gallons after the assumed collection losses. These totals say nothing about reliability during a dry spell. Use local rainfall records and a sequence of inflow, demand and available tank space. Water spilling from a full tank cannot be counted as stored supply.

Step 2: Reduce Debris Before Storage

A first-flush diverter routes an initial portion of runoff away from storage. CDC describes it as a way to improve collected-water quality; it does not establish that the remaining water is drinkable. Diverter sizing depends on the connected roof. Follow the system designer’s specification rather than treating one diversion volume as correct for every roof.

Inspect gutters, screens, the diverter and overflow paths. Keep openings protected against insects and debris, and make sure the diverter resets as intended. Keep rainwater plumbing separate from safe piped drinking water; ask the local authority about required cross-connection protection.

Step 3: Size Usable Storage

Start with measured daily demand and a chosen dry-period scenario. For example, two people using 30 gallons each per day require 2 × 30 × 60 = 3,600 usable gallons for 60 days, before reserves or other uses. This is an arithmetic example, not a recommended consumption allowance or a universal minimum tank size.

Separate garden, livestock and other demand from household demand. Deduct water that cannot be drawn from the tank and plan how you will obtain water if the assumed dry period is exceeded.

Rain Barrels

Small containers can serve limited uses, but calculate their duration. At the example’s 60-gallon daily demand, 550 usable gallons would last about 9.2 days, not 60.

IBC Totes and Reused Containers

Do not treat an unknown-history container as suitable for drinking water because it is advertised as food-grade. Check the actual container, previous contents and intended application with the supplier. CDC says not to use containers that previously held toxic chemicals; see its safe-storage guidance. NSF’s rainwater materials guidance explains evaluation of catchment components and drinking-water-contact materials.

Above-Ground Storage Tanks

Compare usable volume, access, outlet location, support requirements and the manufacturer’s installation instructions. A component’s drinking-water-contact certification addresses materials; it does not certify the safety of the water collected from your roof.

Underground Cisterns

Use a tank intended for burial and a site-specific installation design. Assess excavation, groundwater, access and local requirements before purchase. Burial alone is not a guarantee against freezing or contamination.

Step 4: Choose Treatment for the Actual Contaminants

There is no universal four-stage shopping list that makes every roof supply drinkable. Test the source and choose equipment for the contaminants and intended use. CDC recommends at least annual testing for private well or rainwater supplies and advice from the health department about tests and state-certified laboratories. See choosing home water filters.

Sediment Control

Particle removal can protect downstream equipment. A sediment filter alone does not establish microbiological or chemical safety.

Filter Ratings and Maintenance

Check the exact model’s contaminant-reduction claims and operating limits. Follow its service instructions and monitor performance instead of assuming a fixed replacement interval suits every source.

Chemical Treatment Needs

Choose chemical-reduction equipment against the test results. Taste or odor improvement is not evidence that a harmful contaminant has been removed.

Disinfection Is Different from Filtration

UV treatment can address germs when properly specified and operated, with suitable pre-filtration; it does not remove chemicals. A ceramic or gravity filter is not automatically equivalent to UV and should not be assumed to remove viruses. Compare verified model-specific performance. See CDC’s treatment-method comparison.

Check the Rules for Your Site and Use

Permission to collect runoff does not necessarily permit indoor or drinking-water use. Ask the water-resources authority, health department and local building or plumbing office about the proposed system. The examples below are US agency guidance checked October 23, 2026, not a complete state-by-state legal survey.

  • Colorado: The ordinary residential rain-barrel allowance covers up to two barrels with combined storage of 110 gallons, with conditions on the residence and collection. Uses are outdoors on the collection property, not drinking water or indoor household use. A separate permit route applies to qualifying residential-well properties. Check the Division of Water Resources guidance for eligibility.
  • Utah: The Division of Water Rights describes free registration for total storage up to 2,500 gallons, with collection and use on the same parcel. Registration is not required for no more than two covered containers when neither exceeds 100 gallons. See the official registration guidance.

Common Planning Questions

How much rainwater can I collect from my roof?

Multiply horizontal catchment area in square feet by rainfall in inches, 0.623 and collection efficiency. The 0.623 factor converts units; it does not include losses. Available storage and overflow limit how much you retain.

How much water storage do I need?

Use measured daily demand, a chosen dry period, reserves and unusable tank volume. Two people using 30 gallons each per day need 3,600 usable gallons for 60 days before reserves. This is an example, not a universal minimum.

Is rainwater harvesting legal?

Check the rules for your site, storage size and intended use. Collection permission does not automatically allow indoor or drinking-water use. The Colorado and Utah examples above link to the responsible agencies.

Does a first-flush diverter make rainwater safe to drink?

No. It diverts initial runoff and can improve collected-water quality, but does not prove drinking-water safety. Testing, suitable treatment and maintenance remain necessary.
