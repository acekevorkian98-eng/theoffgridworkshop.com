Plan a Gravity-Fed Water System: Pressure, Flow and Storage

Where Gravity Delivery Fits

An elevated water source can provide pressure without a distribution pump. That does not mean the complete system needs no electricity: filling the tank, boosting pressure or operating treatment equipment may still require power.

The useful question is whether the lowest planned water level can supply the required pressure while the required flow is running. Elevation alone does not establish that a shower, appliance or irrigation device will work. Kansas State’s gravity-system guidance notes that friction losses matter especially in low-pressure systems.

Calculate Static Pressure First

For an open, vented tank of water with no flow, the approximate gauge pressure at a lower point is:

Static pressure (psi) = vertical water-surface elevation above the point (ft) ÷ 2.31

Measure from the water surface, not the tank base, lid or the length of the pipe down the hill. Repeat the calculation for the lowest operating water level and each fixture elevation. The US Bureau of Reclamation explains the pressure/head conversion.

These are not fixture-suitability ratings. Check both the minimum flowing pressure and required flow for the actual fixture or appliance, and the maximum pressure ratings of all components. Lower fixtures can see more static pressure than higher ones.

Illustrative Pressure Budget

Suppose the low water surface is 35 ft above a delivery point. Assume a total flow-related head allowance of 10 ft at the selected flow, including pipe, fittings, treatment and any applicable velocity-head allowance. The remaining pressure head is 25 ft, or about 10.8 psi. If a hypothetical device needs 12 psi at that flow, the example fails the requirement even though its static pressure is 15.2 psi. The loss allowance and device requirement are assumptions, not measured or product-specific data.

Size Usable Storage Around Demand

Measure the water demand served by the tank and choose a refill-interruption scenario. For example, 60 gallons per day for five days requires 300 usable gallons before reserves. Account for water below the outlet, the minimum operating level and other unusable volume.

A three-day or five-day buffer is a planning choice, not a universal standard. Rainwater storage also depends on seasonal supply and dry periods; use the rainwater collection guide alongside the delivery calculation.

Size Pipes Using Flow and Losses

A nominal pipe diameter does not determine one universal maximum flow at 15 psi. Required flow, actual internal diameter, material, length, fittings and elevation all matter. Filters and other equipment consume pressure too.

NC State Extension’s system-head method separates fixture pressure, elevation and friction, and includes losses through fittings and filters. Use data for the actual pipe and components at the chosen flow. Its example pipe tables are not interchangeable with every material or pipe size carrying the same nominal label.

Record these inputs for each critical flow path
InputRecord or Obtain
DemandSimultaneous flows and each device’s required inlet pressure
ElevationLowest and highest water levels relative to the device
PipeMaterial, internal diameter, run length and fittings
TreatmentPressure drop at the design flow and service limit
ResultRemaining pressure at flow, compared with the requirement

Check the demanding paths individually. The highest fixture may have the least elevation head, but a longer or more restricted path may perform worse. If the budget fails, compare a different location, pipe arrangement, lower-demand equipment or a properly selected booster. A pump’s voltage alone does not establish its efficiency or suitability.

Plan Tank Support and Connections

Using terrain avoids building elevation, but the tank still needs suitable support. For perspective, 500 US gallons of water weigh approximately 4,170 lb, using 8.34 lb per gallon, before the tank, fittings and other loads.

Do not use a generic list of lumber sizes as a stand design. Obtain a support and foundation design for the chosen tank, site and loads. Follow its installation instructions. For example, Norwesco specifies full bottom support for its vertical tanks, supported heavy fittings and flexible connections where prescribed. Those instructions are specific to its products, not approval for any improvised platform.

Plan access, overflow routing, isolation, drainage and protection against local freezing conditions. An above-ground tank is not automatically suitable for burial or pressurization. Check the manufacturer’s limits before changing the installation.

Integrate Treatment Without Losing the Pressure Budget

Gravity delivery does not make source water safe. Test the source and select equipment for the relevant contaminants and intended use. CDC’s filter-selection guidance explains why a product’s actual contaminant claims matter.

Verify that treatment equipment can operate at the available inlet pressure and flow. Include its pressure drop in the system calculation, including the service condition specified by the manufacturer. A countertop gravity filter and a pressurized inline treatment unit are different arrangements.

Use materials suitable for the intended water use. NSF describes evaluation of drinking-water-contact tanks and plumbing components; material suitability does not certify the source water. Ask the local authority about cross-connection protection and required plumbing approvals.

Check the Installed System Under Flow

  1. Record source testing, intended uses and applicable local requirements.
  2. Document demand, operating water levels, support design and component ratings.
  3. Complete the pressure budget for each critical path before purchasing equipment.
  4. Have the installation, connections and any protection devices checked as required locally.
  5. Measure static pressure with outlets closed, then measure pressure while the intended simultaneous flows run.
  6. Measure delivered volume over time. Gallons per minute = collected gallons × 60 ÷ elapsed seconds.
  7. Repeat performance checks at the low operating water level and evaluate treatment service limits.
  8. Record results, maintenance intervals and the response to a low-water or treatment fault.

The Bureau of Reclamation’s measurement reference above describes determining flow from known volume and time. A no-flow gauge reading alone does not verify delivery performance.

Common Planning Questions

How much height do I need for a gravity water system?

For an open tank with no flow, divide the water-surface elevation above the point in feet by 2.31 to estimate psi. Required height also depends on losses at the intended flow and the actual device requirements.

What size water tank do I need?

Use measured daily demand and a chosen refill-interruption period, then allow for reserves and unusable volume. The example of 60 gallons per day for five days needs 300 usable gallons before reserves.

Do I need a pump for a gravity water system?

A distribution pump may be unnecessary when elevation supplies the required pressure and flow. Tank filling, treatment or boosting may still need power. Verify the complete system rather than assuming a pump-free design.

What filtration do I need for gravity water?

Choose treatment for the tested source and intended use. Check model-specific contaminant claims, operating pressure and flow, and include treatment losses in the delivery-pressure budget.
