ECO-WORTHY 400W Solar Kit: Which Version, and What Can It Run?

Category: Solar Panels & Kits · Buying guide based on published specifications

By The Off-Grid Workshop

About this page

This is a buying guide, not a hands-on review. We have not tested these kits. It uses ECO-WORTHY's published listings and standard sizing arithmetic to help you choose the right version and check whether 400W is enough for you. An earlier version of this page reported test results and a score that we could not substantiate; they have been removed. How we research.

Quick Answer

"ECO-WORTHY 400W kit" describes several products, not one. All use four 100W, 12V-class panels, but the included charge controller ranges from basic PWM units to a 40A MPPT controller, and some bundles add a lithium battery and an inverter. Before comparing prices, identify which version a listing is.

As a rough guide, 400W of panels delivers about 750 to 1,800Wh per day to your appliances, depending on the month and location (2.5 to 6 peak sun hours, 25% losses). That suits lighting, communications, laptops, small pumps and possibly an efficient fridge, not electric cooking, heating or air conditioning.

What Can a 400W Solar Kit Run?

Panel watts are not daily energy. The useful number is watt-hours per day at your appliances:

Daily Wh ≈ 400W × peak sun hours × (1 − losses)

Peak sun hours are not daylight hours; they depend on location, season and panel angle. Get monthly figures for your site from NREL's free PVWatts calculator and design for the lowest month you need to cover.

An illustrative day at 1,200Wh

  • Six 9W LED bulbs for 5 hours: 270Wh
  • Laptop, 60W for 4 hours: 240Wh
  • Router on 12V DC, 12W for 24 hours: 288Wh
  • Phone charging: 40Wh
  • Water pump, 500W for 30 minutes: 250Wh

That totals 1,088Wh before inverter standby, which can add several hundred watt-hours a day if the inverter stays on. An efficient refrigerator measured at, say, 900Wh per day would need most of the array's output on its own in a 3-to-4 peak-sun-hour month. These figures are illustrations; audit your own loads.

Battery and Inverter Limits

The bundled 12.8V 100Ah battery stores 1,280Wh nominal. With an 80% usable window and 90% delivery efficiency (planning assumptions, not ECO-WORTHY figures), about 920Wh reaches the appliances. That is less than one day of the 1,200Wh example above, with nothing left for a cloudy day. To carry 900Wh a day for two sunless days you would need roughly 2,500Wh nominal, for example two such batteries.

Check battery current against the inverter. An inverter drawing 1,100W from a 12.8V battery at 90% efficiency pulls about 95A. As the battery voltage falls toward its 10V cut-off, the same output needs about 122A. The listed battery maximum is 100A, and motor starts can draw more for a moment. Before running large loads, ask ECO-WORTHY how the battery's protection behaves under sustained high output and surges, or keep continuous AC loads well below the inverter's rating. A 24V system halves these currents.

Cold climates. The listed charging range starts at 0°C. Charging a lithium battery below its minimum temperature can damage it, so install the battery somewhere that stays above freezing, or choose a battery with built-in heating.

Controller, Wiring and Cables

  • MPPT charge current: 400W ÷ 12.8V ≈ 31A, within a 40A controller's rating.
  • Series wiring: panel open-circuit voltage rises in cold weather. Before putting panels in series, multiply the panel Voc by the cold-weather correction for your lowest temperature and keep the string below the controller's maximum PV input voltage. Our sizing guide explains the method.
  • Cable voltage drop: if all four panels are paralleled and their combined current of roughly 20A runs through 16ft of 12AWG cable, the round-trip drop is about 1.3V, around 6% of a 20V array. Shorter runs, heavier cable or series wiring with an MPPT controller reduce this.
  • Protection: check which fuses, breakers and disconnects the kit includes and what your design and local code require. Battery cables in particular need correctly rated overcurrent protection close to the battery.

Who a 400W Kit Suits

  • Likely a good fit: cabins, sheds, workshops, vans and RVs with lighting, devices, small pumps and intermittent tools, where daily use stays roughly within the table above.
  • Likely too small: full-time homes with standard refrigerators and freezers, well pumps with large startup loads, electric cooking, heating or air conditioning. Size from a load audit instead.
  • Before buying any kit: read the actual warranty terms on the listing, including who pays return shipping, and confirm what is and is not in the box.

Frequently Asked Questions

How much power does a 400W solar kit produce per day?

Multiply 400W by your peak sun hours and by one minus your system losses. With 4 peak sun hours and a 25% loss allowance, that is 400 x 4 x 0.75 = 1,200Wh per day at the appliances. In a 2.5 peak-sun-hour winter month it is about 750Wh.

Can a 400W solar kit run a refrigerator?

Often, if the fridge is efficient and the battery is large enough to carry it overnight and through cloudy days. A fridge using 900Wh per day would take most of a 400W array's output in a 3-to-4 peak-sun-hour month. Measure your fridge with an energy meter before relying on a label figure.

Should I choose the PWM or MPPT version?

An MPPT controller lets you wire panels in series within its voltage limit and harvests more energy, especially in cold weather and on longer cable runs. A PWM version is cheaper but requires panels whose operating voltage suits the battery, which usually means wiring 12V panels in parallel. Check the controller included with the exact listing you are buying.

Does the kit include a battery and inverter?

Some ECO-WORTHY 400W kits are panels plus a controller only; others bundle a lithium battery and an inverter. ECO-WORTHY's 400W 12V MPPT bundle lists a 12.8V 100Ah LiFePO4 battery and a 1,100W inverter. Read the 'included in the box' list of the specific listing.

Can I charge the LiFePO4 battery below freezing?

ECO-WORTHY lists a charge temperature range of 0 to 55 degrees C for the battery in its 400W MPPT bundle, so charging below freezing is outside that range. In cold climates, keep the battery in a heated space or choose a battery with built-in low-temperature heating.
