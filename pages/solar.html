Solar Power for Off-Grid Living

Plan around measured loads, the solar resource, usable storage and compatible equipment. Start with the audit, record the assumptions and check the complete system before buying.

Start With a Power Audit Size Your System

What the Main Components Do

A battery-based solar system needs generation, charging control, storage and delivery to its loads. Separate devices may provide these functions, or an integrated product may combine them. The exact arrangement depends on the design.

  1. Solar panels produce DC electricity. A watt rating describes output under specified test conditions, not a constant output or a promise of daily energy. Assess the site, season, shading, orientation and system losses.
  2. Charging equipment regulates energy going into the battery. Match its profile and current limits to the actual battery, and verify PV input and temperature-protection requirements. PWM and MPPT describe controller approaches; neither label alone proves lithium compatibility. Renogy documents lithium support on a specific Wanderer Li PWM model.
  3. The battery bank stores energy and must meet both energy and current demands. Record nominal voltage and Ah, or nominal Wh, alongside usable discharge, delivery losses, temperature limits and protection. See the battery comparison guide.
  4. An inverter supplies AC from the DC system where AC loads need it. Check output voltage/frequency, continuous ratings in applicable watts and VA, startup capability and duration, idle use and appliance compatibility. DC loads may use a different delivery path.

The full installation also needs suitable mounting, wiring, protection, isolation and monitoring. Start with the loads and site, then check component compatibility together; a kit’s headline wattage does not establish a complete design.

Record Your Sizing Inputs

A van, cabin or home label does not determine the system size. These are the inputs to collect before comparing equipment:

Planning inputs, rather than preset equipment or price recommendations
InputWhat to RecordWhere to Start
Daily load energyWh per day, seasonal variation and essential loadsPower audit
Simultaneous AC demandRunning watts/VA and startup magnitude and durationDevice data and measurements
Solar resourceLocation, design month, orientation and shadingSite assessment and local resource data
Storage durationChosen autonomy, reserve and backup or load-reduction planYour reliability requirements
Battery energyNominal voltage and Wh/Ah, usable fraction and delivery lossesModel datasheet and battery guide
Equipment limitsPV voltage/current, charge control, temperature and protection requirementsAll component manuals

Keep power and energy separate: a 100 W load running for three hours uses 300 Wh. A 600 W load used for five minutes uses 50 Wh, yet requires more running power while it is on. Motor startup can add a separate requirement.

Use the solar sizing calculator with explicit assumptions, then verify the proposed equipment and installation. For costs, compare current quotes that include the same scope, protection, installation and backup provisions; a generic percentage is not an installation estimate.

Four Checks Before Buying Equipment

  1. Reliability: choose autonomy from the site's weather, essential demand and backup plan. There is no universal three-to-five-day battery requirement.
  2. Compatibility: confirm the full charging profile, battery controls, PV limits and device operating ranges. A PWM/MPPT label or chemistry preset is not sufficient evidence.
  3. Cables: check current capacity, total circuit length, voltage drop, installation conditions and manufacturer requirements. A single loss percentage does not describe performance at every load.
  4. Protection: specify overcurrent protection and isolation for the actual system. Fuse selection includes current, DC voltage and interrupt capability; it is not just a matter of choosing an amp rating.

Victron’s DC wiring reference explains cable and protection considerations. Use equipment instructions and applicable local requirements for the final design and commissioning.

For broader planning context, see the Australian Government’s solar design considerations and the California Energy Commission’s component overview. These describe planning principles; local installation requirements still need checking.
